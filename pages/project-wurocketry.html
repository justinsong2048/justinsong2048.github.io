Work / WURocketry

WURocketry nosecone retention

A Delrin retention plate and a 3D-printed PLA nosecone shoulder that hold the nosecone and payload together through launch and recovery, for Washington University Rocketry's NASA Student Launch entry.

The problem

Hold it together, then let go on command

The payload rides in the nosecone section. When the recovery parachute opens, the harness yanks that section hard, and the retention system has to survive the jerk without the nosecone separating or the payload shifting.

I calculated that snatch load with the team's 25× rule of thumb: 25 times the rocket's 42.4 lb dry weight (its weight after motor burnout, when the parachute deploys) is about 4.7 kN, which I rounded up to a 5,000 N design load. The shoulder also had to keep a sliding fit inside the body tube, and the whole assembly had to stay light.

Requirements
  • Survive a 5,000 N snatch load with margin
  • Keep the payload electronics fixed under launch and landing shock
  • Slide-fit into the airframe body tube
  • Release on command, using solenoid pins
Approach

Design, analysis, build, test

1 · Design

Choosing a retention method

I compared latches and hooks, Marman bands and solenoid pins, and the team scored the options in weighted decision matrices. Solenoid pins won: the team had used them the previous season, and they release on an electrical signal.

The plate carries both solenoids and bolts to the shoulder. I detailed it in a SolidWorks drawing to ASME Y14.5, with an overall envelope of 5.64 × 3.50 in at 1:2 scale.

I detailed the shoulder in its own drawing, with a section view through the solenoid pin holes to show the internal recess and the insert bores.

2 · Analysis

FEA on the shoulder

In SolidWorks Simulation, I applied the 5,000 N snatch load to the shoulder's upper face, where it attaches to the nosecone. I held the part with fixed-hinge fixtures at the two places it is actually restrained in the rocket. The first is the side holes, where the solenoid pins pass through the shoulder. The second is the heat-set inserts, where the retention plate screws in.

I used a fine mesh so the von Mises stress would read accurately around tight features like hole edges and corners, where it concentrates.

Peak von Mises stress was 14.9 MPa, at the fastener holes. Against the 27.5 MPa yield strength I used for PLA, that's a 1.8 safety factor. The shoulder was printed upright, with its layers stacked along the rocket's axis, so the snatch load pulls across the layer lines, PLA's weakest direction. The simulation treats the PLA as equally strong in every direction, so the real margin is likely below 1.8.

3 · Build

Machining and printing the parts

I machined the plate from Delrin stock, combining manual milling with CNC operations that I programmed at the machine with a shop technician. I worked with the structures subteam to hold the plate on their jig. I also drilled the body-tube mounting holes, leveling the tube on balancing blocks so the holes landed where the drawing said.

The shoulder was 3D-printed in PLA, with heat-set inserts in its bolt holes for the retention plate screws, and fits into the base of the nosecone.

4 · Test

Testing and assembly

The assembly passed 25-ft drop tests, coming out with no cracks or deformation that compromised the structure. Along the way, I took the shoulder through 3 revisions. I added fillets to remove the sharp corners where stress concentrated, and corrected the hole fit on the retention plate.

Assembly was the hardest part. The recessed cut in the shoulder that holds the solenoids left little clearance, so fitting the solenoids in with their pins was very tight.

Outcome

Written up, reviewed and accepted by NASA

I wrote the retention sections of the team's NASA Student Launch proposal, which NASA accepted, and of the Preliminary Design Review. I also presented the design at cross-team design reviews. I'm now mentoring a new member through the same CAD and machining process. The team didn't complete the subscale launches the competition required, so the payload didn't fly.

What I'd change: I'd give the recessed extruded cut in the shoulder more clearance, so the solenoids and their pins go in easily during assembly. I'd also print and pull-test a coupon in the same orientation to measure PLA's strength across layers, and check the shoulder against that number instead.
