Work / Ghost Armoire

Ghost Armoire door mechanism

A trick armoire for the stage. A performer inside releases a latch, and constant-force springs swing the doors open with no motor and no sound. One stagehand resets them between scenes.

0Motors. The springs do all the work, silently.
7.7 sTo an assumed 140° stop with the 3.29 lb spring, inside the 6–10 s target
1Stagehand resets both doors
The problem

Doors that open by themselves, on cue

The doors have to open as if no one touched them. The performer inside triggers them, so there's no visible stagehand and no motor noise, and one crew member has to reset them between scenes without tools.

Requirements
  • Silent, with no motor
  • Triggered from inside by the performer
  • One stagehand can reset it
  • Opens in 6–10 s
  • Opens to at least 135°, ideally 150°
Approach

Concept, sizing, motion check, reset

1 · Concept

Store energy, release on cue

A constant-force spring stores the energy to open each door, and an aluminum wire-frame latch holds the springs back until the cue. The performer pushes the top of the wire frame, which lifts it out of its mounting hole and frees the springs.

Each spring turns a horizontal shaft, but the door swings about a vertical hinge. So each door gets a pair of 1:1 miter gears that turn the rotation 90°. A 1:1 ratio keeps the spring's torque equal to the hinge torque, so the Step 2 sizing applies straight to the door. The door only needs 5/12 of a turn to reach 150°, so there's nothing to gear up or down. Each gear runs on a McMaster 57155K355 ball bearing with the same 1/4 in bore, so both mount on one shaft.

2 · Sizing

Sizing the spring, checking the gears

The required torque comes from the door's moment of inertia about the hinge and the angular acceleration needed to open it in 8 s, the middle of the 6–10 s target.

α = 2θ / t²
τreq = I · α
τspring = F · rdrum ≥ τreq θ = 150° (2.62 rad), t = 8 s → α ≈ 0.082 rad/s² · I ≈ 1.50 kg·m² (22 lb mahogany door plus gear train, from the motion study; hand check: a uniform 22 lb door 0.64 m wide, turning about its edge, gives m·w²/3 ≈ 1.37 kg·m², within 10%) → τreq ≈ 0.12 N·m

A constant-force spring pulls at the radius of its drum, and the drum has to be 10–20% larger than the spring's inner diameter, so force and drum size come as a pair. I compared catalog springs by the torque each gives on its own drum:

  • 1.48 lb, 0.45 in ID, 0.5 in drum: 0.042 N·m. Far too weak.
  • 2.63 lb, 0.59 in ID, 0.70 in drum: 0.105 N·m. Short of 0.12 N·m.
  • 3.29 lb, 0.61 in ID, 0.67 in drum: 0.125 N·m. Meets it. Selected (McMaster 9293K116).

With 1:1 gears the drum turns as far as the door, so a 140° swing rewinds 0.82 in of strip (0.335 in × 2.44 rad). The spring reaches its rated force after about 1.25 drum diameters of extension (0.84 in), so it's pre-extended at least 1.7 in with the door closed and pulls at full force all the way open.

Gears and bearings. McMaster doesn't publish a torque rating for the 6529K53 miter gear, so I checked tooth bending with the Lewis equation at the gear's mean radius, using the bevel gear's equivalent tooth count (24 / cos 45° ≈ 34 teeth).

Ft = τ / rmean
σ = Ft · Pmean / (b · Y) τ = 0.125 N·m (1.10 in·lb), rmean = 0.43 in → Ft ≈ 2.6 lb · Pmean ≈ 28 teeth/in, b = 13/64 in, Y ≈ 0.37 → σ ≈ 960 psi, against a yield strength of at least 50 ksi for unhardened 1144 steel: safety factor over 50

The same mesh puts about 2.7 lb radial and 0.7 lb axial load on each gear's bearing. The door's weight goes into the hinges, not these bearings.

3 · Motion check

Tested in SolidWorks Motion

I modeled the spring as a constant 0.125 N·m torque on the driving miter gear. Gear and lock mates carry it to the left door, which starts closed; the right door has the same mechanism. The study has no friction or contact. The cabinet as modeled stops the door at 122°, so I assumed a 140° door stop, inside the 135–150° target.

  • The door reaches 140° in 7.7 s, inside the 6–10 s target, at about 36°/s.
  • Friction only slows the door, so it can't open faster than 7.7 s. Reaching 140° in 10 s needs just 0.073 N·m, which leaves about 0.05 N·m (40% of the spring's torque) for hinge friction.

Two problems remain:

  • The 140° stop is assumed. The cabinet as modeled stops the door at 122°, so it has to be changed where it meets the door.
  • The spring keeps accelerating the door, so it hits the stop at about 36°/s. That's a slam, not the slow, eerie swing the scene needs.

Friction, for scale: if the hinges behave like mahogany on mahogany, the static coefficient is about 0.25–0.5 and the kinetic about 0.20. The closer the spring's torque is to the kinetic friction torque, the closer the door comes to a steady swing instead of speeding into the stop.

4 · Reset and docs

One-person reset

A Y-shaped rope tied to a hook on the bottom of each door lets one stagehand pull both doors closed and recharge both springs in one motion. I documented the mechanism in a bill of materials (Table 1) and SolidWorks drawings.

Outcome

What came out of it

The armoire was never built for the stage. The project ended with the design, the drawings and the SolidWorks Motion study.

What I'd change:

  • Add tracks along the cabinet's inner wall to keep the spring extended. This was only a simulation, and I suspect the spring would curl up as soon as it was released.
  • Calculate the hinge friction from the forces on the door and confirm it stays under the roughly 0.05 N·m the spring can spare.
